Seeing Myself

A Social Justice 12 Inquiry into Six Lived Social Identities

Identity is often treated as something we completely choose for ourselves. But when we look at the actual evidence in our daily lives, identity is shaped by the society and systems around us. We are grouped, viewed, and affected by institutions and social norms that give privilege to some while creating barriers for others.

In this Social Justice 12 project, I explore six social identities: socio-economic status, religion, language, ability, age, and gender, using real personal evidence from my life. From getting my PC parts after asking for over a year, to recovering from eye surgery as a kid, each section compares outward appearances with what was really happening behind the scenes, connecting my personal story directly with what we are studying in class.

Interpretation: High-Spec Hardware vs. The "Rich as Hell" Myth

Evidence 1 shows the boxes for my custom desktop PC build (Intel Core i7-13700K processor, MSI Z790 motherboard, 32GB DDR5 RAM, and RTX 4070 SUPER inside an NZXT H7 Elite case). My personal journey with computer hardware began back in Grade 8: for over a year, I spent hours every afternoon watching build guides, learning part synergies, and dreaming about building my own computer.

When friends and peers see a PC of that spec running on my desk, their immediate reaction is to assume I am "rich as hell." They see thousands of dollars of hardware and assume our family has disposable wealth or effortless financial comfort. But interpreting this evidence through our actual socioeconomic reality reveals the complete opposite: our family relies on a single income in Metro Vancouver, which is one of the most expensive housing markets in North America. My parents did not buy this computer because we had cash to throw around; they bought it because they saw my genuine dedication over twelve months, choosing to absorb an immense household financial burden to invest in my passion.

Complication: Parental Sacrifice and the Time Spent Obsessed

What an outside observer never sees from looking at a completed workstation or a Task Manager screenshot is how much my dad had to work to buy me that PC, and how much time I spent completely obsessed about building it. For more than a year, I spent nearly all my free time watching component teardowns, benchmark comparisons, and PCPartPicker pricing charts, constantly trying to cut costs, find combo discounts, and shave every possible dollar off the total build.

When my parents finally said "screw it" and bought the components in 2024, they secretly hoped that buying the PC would end my obsession with hardware videos once and for all. But they could not have been more wrong: getting the machine sparked an even deeper obsession with coding, systems architecture, and technology. At the same time, opening those component boxes brought a complicated mix of joy and quiet guilt. In a single-income immigrant household, buying expensive tech means delaying other family savings, sacrificing parents' personal comforts, and carrying hidden stress about unforeseen emergencies. High-end hardware on the surface often hides steep economic strain underneath.

Connection: Effort vs. Result Discussion and Meritocracy

This personal experience connects directly to our class Effort vs. Result discussion and the core frameworks from our EQUITY and ACCESS presentations. In class, our Effort vs. Result discussion centered on the societal belief that if you just put in enough effort, you will automatically achieve positive results. But in reality, effort does not equal result when people face unequal starting points.

In our Equity presentation, we analyzed how meritocracy is flawed: "Meritocracy disregards systemic advantages and disadvantages. People from different backgrounds do not start out from the same level (educational, social, economic) but are inherently privileged by the quality of their education, social networks and economic leverage." We explored this during "The Scholarship" class activity, debating whether achievement should be judged purely by outcome or by the barriers students face. Access to technology is an essential educational tool, yet as our Access presentation emphasized, financial barriers prevent lower-income and working-class students from obtaining necessary hardware. The baseball fence visual from class illustrated that equality (giving everyone the same box) is not equity (giving people what they need based on their circumstances) or justice (removing systemic barriers entirely). My ability to learn advanced computing required my dad working extra hard to absorb a heavy financial stretch, proving that personal effort alone does not guarantee equal opportunity without financial leverage.

Questions & Shifts: Effort vs. Result in an Unequal Society

"How to create a society where effort = result?"

Throughout this inquiry, my thinking underwent a major shift regarding the relationship between effort and outcome. Before, I viewed my PC build purely as an individual achievement: I put in the effort to research parts, so I got the result. But connecting this to our class discussions on meritocracy and our Effort vs. Result debate made me realize that effort alone is not enough. A working-class immigrant parent can put in twice the physical effort of an executive, yet systemic economic barriers still keep financial security and homeownership out of reach. On the other hand, wealthy families achieve results with minimal effort simply through economic leverage. My shift was realizing that to create a society where effort genuinely equals result, we must eliminate structural financial barriers, guarantee universal access to educational technology, and ensure no student's potential is capped by their family's bank account.

Complication: Parents' Strong Belief vs. Shifting Toward Atheism

When I was younger, my parents were pretty high on religious belief and that naturally rubbed off on me. As a young child, I accepted rituals and prayers without question because they were the air I breathed at home. But as I grew older and started viewing the world logically through science and analytical reasoning, I slowly shifted toward an atheist mindset, though not fully. Today, I do not believe in supernatural deities, or divine intervention.

The complication is that someone looking at my Tirupati temple pilgrimage photo would never see this internal shift. If I were to stubbornly refuse to wear a dhoti or argue against family prayers, I would not be proving intellectual independence; I would simply be hurting my parents, who sacrificed everything to immigrate to Canada and for whom these rituals provide profound emotional peace and grounding. Participating in ceremonies while quietly holding an atheist mindset is not hypocrisy; it is an intentional choice made in love, respect for my parents, and family closeness. The tension lies in balancing intellectual skepticism with deep empathy for the traditions that comfort my family.

Connection: Age, Experience, and Viewpoints on Religion

This dynamic connects directly to how age impacts maturity of belief, personal life experience, and how one's viewpoint changes due to religion, as examined in our class units on DIVERSITY, HUMAN RIGHTS, and WHY INJUSTICE HAPPENS.

In class, we explored how age impacts maturity of belief: as children mature into adolescents, cognitive development pushes them to question inherited principles rather than passively absorbing them. Life experience, such as moving from India to Canada and encountering multicultural perspectives, reshapes our worldview. In our Diversity unit, we studied the Three Dimensions of Diversity: primary demographic diversity (born into a Hindu family), secondary experiential diversity (migrating and practicing temple rituals), and tertiary cognitive diversity (my logical skepticism and analytical problem-solving). Our class also explored "Mirrors vs. Windows": finding cultural reflection in temple spaces while viewing different belief systems through a respectful window. Furthermore, in our Human Rights unit, we studied the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms and Eleanor Roosevelt's work on the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948), which protects freedom of conscience as an essential private civil liberty. In Why Injustice Happens, Maslow's hierarchy showed that humans have a core psychological need for belonging and love. Honoring my family's traditions fulfills that essential need for belonging against cultural isolation.

Questions & Shifts: Coexistence of Religion and Logic

"Is there a fine point where religion and logic can co-exist?"

My thinking underwent a deep shift regarding this exact question. In early adolescence, after shifting away from my parents' religious influence, I believed that logic and religion were completely incompatible enemies, and that being rational meant constantly debunking superstitions and arguing with family. But through our Social Justice 12 lessons on diversity, nurture, and psychological needs, I discovered that fine point where religion and logic can co-exist: logic governs my understanding of the natural, scientific world, while religious ritual serves as an emotional language of familial love, communal respect, and cultural unity. I do not need to believe in miracles to stand beside my parents in a temple; love and respect for my family are entirely logical reasons to participate.

Complication: Inability to Read Mother Tongue and Being Treated Differently

A major personal complication that is not visible from textbook photos or airport pictures was that because all my formal schooling in Chennai was conducted in Tamil and English, I couldn't read or write in my own mother tongue (Telugu), even though I spoke it fluently at home with my parents. This created an internal complication: feeling culturally anchored to a language that I could only speak orally but could not write or read.

The second complication arose when I came to Canada: being treated differently and facing persistent judgments about first language. Canadian society constantly flattens the rich diversity of India into a single stereotype, assuming that every Indian must speak Hindi or Punjabi. Classmates and acquaintances routinely ask: "Do you speak Hindi or Punjabi?" When I explain that my mother tongue is Telugu, people often look confused or dismissive. It is isolating to have your first language treated like an oddity or an outsider simply because it does not fit mainstream Canadian assumptions about South Asian culture.

This experience connects directly to our class discussions on Judgement about first language and the core frameworks from our SOCIAL IDENTITY, PRIVILEGE AND OPPRESSION, and DIVERSITY presentations.

In class, we discussed how judgment about first language operates as a tool of marginalization: people are judged, mocked, or stereotyped based on their accent, mother tongue, or English fluency. In our Social Identity presentation, Slide 10 explicitly analyzed Ethnicity vs. National Origin: "Ethnicity is based on shared culture, language, ancestry, and traditions, whereas National Origin is defined by political borders, citizenship, and country of birth." My national origin is Indian by birth and Canadian by citizenship, but my ethnic and linguistic identity is specifically Telugu and South Indian. In Privilege and Oppression, we studied how Stereotypes ("all Indians speak Hindi/Punjabi") lead to Prejudice (preconceptions about what South Asians should sound like), Discrimination, and Oppression. Slide 28 emphasized that "privilege often feels like ignorance": speakers of dominant languages never have to deal with having their native tongue dismissed or erased from public awareness.

I used to feel self-conscious and frustrated that I could not read or write in Telugu, and annoyed when people in Canada judged my first language. But adopting a framework of effort helped me realize that knowing more languages rather than perfecting only one lets me communicate with and understand far more people. Language exists to connect people, not to be spoken perfectly. Speaking Telugu at home connects me to my roots and honors my mother's two years of solo parenting in Chennai, while knowing Tamil and English allows me to build bridges with different communities across Canada.

Complication: Stress, Temporary Blindness, and Reliance on Others

Looking at an old photograph of childhood surgery, it is easy to view it as a simple medical story with a quick recovery. What remains invisible beneath the photo is the intense stress and the harsh negatives of being blind and having total reliance on those around you.

As a young child, waking up unable to see clearly brought visceral panic and fear. Every single basic action required total dependency: being spoon-fed every bite of food, being guided by holding my parents' forearms just to take two steps, and sitting in darkness to avoid burning eye pain. The frustration of wanting to move freely while being trapped in dependency was overwhelming. More deeply, reflecting on that week as a Grade 12 student made me realize that while my blindness was temporary, millions of people live with permanent physical and sensory disabilities every day. For them, the hardest struggle is not just their physical impairment; it is living in an ableist society that looks down on reliance, treats accommodations as burdens, and criticizes people who need assistance.

Connection: Mental and Physical Disability Discussions and Accommodations

This reflection connects directly to our class discussions about mental disability and the accommodation of disabilities, alongside our presentations on PARTICIPATION, ACCESS, and PRIVILEGE AND OPPRESSION.

In class, our discussions about mental disability and accommodations highlighted how society views unimpaired, neurotypical bodies and minds as the universal default. Accommodations (whether ramps, screen readers, extra testing time, or mental health days) are frequently resented or treated as special privileges rather than basic human rights. In our Participation unit, we explored positionality and the critical distinction between "centering voices" versus "consulting voices." Slide 8 examined Scenario 1: "Disability Rights and Accessibility in Public Spaces - A new public transit system lacks adequate accessibility for people with mobility challenges. Whose voices should be centred? Individuals with disabilities and disability rights activists! Whose voices should be consulted? City officials, engineers, planners." In Privilege and Oppression, Slide 46 broke down "Stereotype #2: People with disabilities are helpless or incapable." This stereotype feeds the cycle of oppression, teaching society to pity disabled people rather than removing architectural and institutional barriers.

Questions & Shifts: Recognizing the Universal Spectrum of Ability

"At what point will society realize we're all on the spectrum?"

My thinking about ability and disability underwent a major transformation through this project. Before, I thought of disability as a fixed, separate category that only applied to certain people. But my childhood surgery and our class discussions helped me realize that ability, mental health, and physical need exist on a continuous spectrum. Every human being is temporarily abled: we enter life completely dependent on caregivers, we experience illness, injury, and mental health struggles, and we age into physical vulnerability. Asking "At what point will society realize we're all on the spectrum?" means recognizing that interdependence is universal. Once society understands that everyone sits on this spectrum, accommodations will no longer be treated as inconvenient exceptions, but as the foundational architecture of an equitable community.

Society creates formal benchmarks and assigns adult expectations the moment a person reaches a certain birthday or earns a plastic card.

Complication: Parents Withholding Problems and the Ageist Double Standard

The first major complication around age in my life was that when I was younger, my parents deliberately withheld problems, financial worries, and household stress from me. They kept quiet about the steep challenges of immigration and single-income finances to protect my childhood. It was only as I grew older that they began gradually sharing what sort of problems we were facing, such as being unable to buy a house even after eight years in Canada because only my dad is working.

This dynamic connects directly to the central contradiction of adolescent age, which I call the ageist double standard: "Well, I'm an adult if the situation we're talking about needs me to be an adult, and a child otherwise." Society constantly uses age as a double standard to control youth. When adults need teenagers to take on heavy chores, perform academically, or bear legal liability, they demand: "You're seventeen, act like an adult!" But the second a young person questions unfair rules, asserts their autonomy, or demands a seat at the table, adults dismiss them: "You're just a child, you don't understand the real world." Age is used as a convenient switch flipped by adults to demand adult responsibility while withholding adult respect.

Connection: Age Dependence != Maturity Discussion and Institutional Power

This dynamic connects directly to our class discussion on how Age dependence != Maturity, alongside our concepts from SOCIAL IDENTITY, PRIVILEGE AND OPPRESSION, and WHY INJUSTICE HAPPENS.

In class, our "Age dependence != Maturity" discussion unpacked how legal systems and schools enforce dependence on youth based on age, confusing chronological age with actual maturity. In our Social Identity presentation, Slide 6 broke down Age into categories (Child 0-12, Teen 13-17, Young Adult 18-26, Adult 27-64, Senior 65+) and prompted us to examine: "How is someone's identity affected by their age? What role does age play in how others see and treat us?" Age is an assigned identity linked to systemic power. In Privilege and Oppression, we studied how ageism operates as an institutional system where adults hold authority over youth. Slide 28 noted that "having privilege often feels like ignorance," explaining why adults rarely recognize their age privilege. In Why Injustice Happens, Slides 10-11 showed that teenagers have an intense psychological need to be respected, trusted, and feel belonging. When ageism denies young people agency, it produces alienation and reinforces arbitrary age hierarchies.

Questions & Shifts: Determining Maturity Beyond Chronological Age

"How does one determine maturity now that we know age has no relationship with it?"

Throughout this project, my understanding of maturity shifted completely. When I got my Class 7L permit, I bought into the societal myth that age 16 automatically made me mature. But our class discussions on "Age dependence != Maturity" and ageism helped me realize that chronological age has no true relationship with maturity. Passing a multiple-choice driving test or turning sixteen does not bestow wisdom. Real maturity cannot be measured by a birth certificate, a driver's licence, or being in Grade 12; it is determined by empathy, economic transparency, the ability to support family through financial hurdles, and taking genuine responsibility for those around you. Realizing this freed me from chasing arbitrary age status symbols.

Complication: Relative Teasing and the Social Construction of Masculinity

Because my cousin was born before me, clothes were passed down naturally, but over time, I noticed being teased differently by relatives about wearing a dress as a toddler. At family gatherings, relatives still bring out the photograph of me in the hand-me-down frock and tease me affectionately about it.

When you look beneath the surface, that innocent toddler photo exposes a profound social complication: Why is a two-year-old boy in a patterned frock seen as adorable, but if a seventeen-year-old male wore that same garment to school today, he would be subjected to severe bullying, mockery, or physical violence? Between age two and age seventeen, society strictly conditions children into gender boxes. Boys are pressured to conform to rigid masculine stereotypes: showing no emotion, never wearing bright colors, and asserting physical toughness. I was fortunate that both my school and home environments provided safety and support, allowing me to be whatever I want without feeling forced to prove my masculinity through toxic behavior or expensive brand-name fashion. The complication lies in recognizing how early gender policing begins and how much pressure society places on young men to suppress their individuality.

Connection: "The Dress Like the Opposite Sex" Video, Sex vs. Gender, and Learned Behavior

This inquiry connects directly to "The dress like the opposite sex" video we watched and analyzed in class, as well as our presentations on SOCIAL IDENTITY, PRIVILEGE AND OPPRESSION, and WHY INJUSTICE HAPPENS. In the video, parents dressed toddlers in clothes traditionally associated with the other sex (dressing a boy in a dress, or a girl in boy's clothes), and researchers observed how caregivers and adults at nurseries interacted with them. The experiment showed that caregivers immediately and instinctively handed certain toys to boys (like construction trucks, blocks, and tools) and certain toys to girls (like dolls, soft plushies, and tea sets) as second nature, whether they did it consciously on purpose or unconsciously by accident.

This experiment highlights the critical distinction from our Social Identity presentation between biological sex (Slide 12: chromosomes, hormones, and anatomy assigned at birth) and socially constructed gender (Slide 14: social roles, clothing, behaviors, and expectations). Even though the video is titled "The dress like the opposite sex," what was really being tested was gender socialization and presentation: clothes and toys have no biological sex; they are given gendered meanings by society.

This experiment connects directly to our presentation on WHY INJUSTICE HAPPENS (Slides 18-21) on Nurture: "Injustices are believed, practiced and perpetuated because we have been TAUGHT this from a young age... our participation is a LEARNED behaviour." Toddlers do not naturally police gender or assign roles to toys and fabrics; to a two-year-old in a hand-me-down frock, clothing is purely functional and comfortable. It is caregivers and nursery environments that treat gender scripts as second nature, channeling kids into gender expectations from infancy. In PRIVILEGE AND OPPRESSION (Slides 42-45), the Cycle of Oppression and Internalization explains how young boys often internalize rigid masculine expectations to avoid teasing and maintain belonging (Slide 10). Recognizing that gender roles are learned social expectations gives us the power to reject performative stereotypes, choose casual clothes, and stay authentic to who we are in all our daily interactions.

Questions & Shifts: Self-Representation in Interactions

"Does self-representation create the most memorable first impression? And what role does it play in interactions?"

When I was younger, I used to feel slightly embarrassed whenever family members brought up the picture of me in my cousin's frock. Through our Social Justice 12 lessons on sex versus gender, nurture as learned behavior, and the nursery video experiment on opposite gender socialization (where caregivers automatically handed out toys based on how toddlers were dressed), my understanding transformed. I now view that photograph with pride as living proof that gender norms are socially constructed. Today, I dress casually in hoodies and focus on who I am rather than worrying about performative fashion or gender expectations. How we choose to present ourselves creates memorable first impressions and influences how people interact with us, but true confidence comes from focusing on who I am as a person rather than worrying about what other people assume.

From my passion starting in Grade 8, seeing over a year of asking my parents for PC parts watching build videos, to when they actually bought it in 2024, people assumed I was "rich as hell." In actuality, it was a big financial sacrifice for our single-income family. As I grew older, my parents became more transparent about our financial struggles and inability to even buy a house after 8 years in Canada on a single income. Learning this taught me what it means to be economically sacrificing.

Social Justice Connection

Working-class immigrant youth often navigate a complex reality: having access to digital tools while gradually learning the steep economic costs absorbed by parents in silence. Maturity develops when generational silence is replaced with economic transparency.

Key Takeaway: "Growing up isn't about buying expensive things; it's understanding what your parents gave up to support you."
